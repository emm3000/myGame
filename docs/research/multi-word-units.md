# Evidence brief: multi-word units and example frames for the curriculum

Compiled 2026-11-12 for the ADR on multi-word units (#1001). Verification rule as in `english-pivot/research.md`: a claim is **verified** when it was read in the primary paper, the publisher's page, a Crossref record carrying the publisher's abstract, or the authors' own list file. **Secondary, unverified** marks anything seen only in a summary, a citing paper or a search snippet. Licence statements are what the hosting page says; nothing here is legal advice.

## 1. PHRASE List (Martinez & Schmitt, 2012)

- **Count.** "505 multiword items" in the paper (verified, paper text). A parse of the authors' `.doc` appendix found 506 rows; per 1K band 32 / 84 / 129 / 158 / 103 (the paper says 32 and 85 for the first two bands). Treat 505 as the published figure and the one-row difference as unexplained.
- **Corpus.** The 100-million-word BNC. WordSmith extracted every 2–4-word n-gram repeated at least 5 times (4.2 million); only n-grams with at least **787** occurrences were kept, because 787 is the frequency of the 5,000th BNC word family. About 15,000 candidates were then hand-checked (verified, paper pp. 310–311).
- **Selection.** Frequency, a "discrete, identifiable meaning or function", and relative **non-compositionality**: transparent sequences such as *at all times* were excluded, opaque ones such as *at all* kept. Corpus-informed dictionaries and an external rater confirmed items (verified, paper pp. 303–304, 311; User's Guide).
- **Frequency bands.** Each item is placed in the **1K band of BNC word families** whose frequency it matches (1K–5K), and its "Integrated List Rank" is where it would fall in a merged list of single word families and phrases (ranks run 107 to 5,504 in the file) (verified, `.doc` header and User's Guide). The bands are BNC word-family bands, not GSL or AWL bands; the GSL and AWL appear only as earlier single-word lists in the abstract (verified). Of the component words, 95% sit in the first 1,000 families and 2.88% in the second (verified, paper p. 313). These bands are not the same as NGSL lemma bands; mapping a PHRASE band onto an NGSL band would be our approximation.
- **British.** Yes. The authors call the BNC "mostly British, skewed towards written English" (paper p. 310), and the User's Guide says the list "would be of very limited use for someone wanting to know which phrases are most common in spoken American casual conversation" because the BNC is "mostly written British English only as modern as the early 1990s" (verified).
- **Columns in the file.** Integrated List Rank, Phrase, Frequency (per 100 million, lemmatised, counting only the non-transparent sense), Spoken general, Written general, Written academic (stars: `***` most common genre down to `x` rare), Example (one sentence) (verified, `.doc`).
- **Meanings.** **No definitions or senses.** Some entries carry a disambiguating tag, e.g. `GET TO ('ARRIVE AT')` and `GET TO ('OPPORTUNITY')`, `GOING TO (FUTURE)` (verified, `.doc`).
- **Phrasal verbs are included, not excluded.** Neither the paper's core criteria nor the User's Guide exclude phrasal verbs; the Guide names them as one formulaic type among others, and the list keeps any opaque one that passes the frequency floor (verified, paper pp. 308–309; Guide). A heuristic count of the `.doc` (verb + particle or preposition) finds about 80 such entries, prepositional verbs included: GO ON, DEAL WITH, SET UP, CARRY OUT, PICK UP, FIND OUT, POINT OUT, WORK OUT, TURN OUT, GIVE UP, END UP, COME UP WITH, GET AWAY WITH (count is ours, approximate). Extraction used contiguous 2–4-grams, so a split use (*pick it up*) was probably not counted toward the frequency; the paper was not checked on that point. A PHRASE + PHaVE curriculum would duplicate these items.
- **Download.** norbertschmitt.co.uk/vocabulary-resources links the list (`.doc`), a User's Guide (`.doc`) and the article PDF (verified).
- **Licence.** **None stated.** The resources page gives no licence, copyright or commercial-use terms for the list; "freely available for research and pedagogical purposes" on that page refers to the Vocabulary Levels Tests, not to the lists (verified). The article carries "© Oxford University Press 2012" and calls the list "online supplementary data" (verified, PDF p. 299 and p. 313). With no licence, the default is all rights reserved: the examples and the file cannot go into a CC BY-SA directory. Which phrases appear, with their counts, are corpus facts; writing our own glosses and frames for items chosen with the list as a guide is a different act from copying the file, but whether a selection this curated is itself protected (and whether the UK database right may cover extracting it, the lists being published from the UK) was not checked.

Ref: Martinez, R., & Schmitt, N. (2012). A phrasal expressions list. *Applied Linguistics, 33*(3), 299–320. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/ams010 (verified, full text and list file read)

## 2. PHaVE List (Garnier & Schmitt, 2015)

- **Count.** 150 phrasal verbs, **288 meaning senses**, 1.92 senses per verb on average (verified, paper p. 660; a count of the list PDF also gives 150 headwords and 288 percentage-tagged senses).
- **Which 150.** Taken unchanged from **Liu (2011)**: phrasal verbs with at least 10 tokens per million in COCA **or** the BNC, out of 8,847 searched; 152 items, merged to 150 (look around/round, turn around/round). Those 150 cover 62.95% of the 512,305 PV occurrences in the BNC (verified, paper pp. 648, 651–652).
- **Senses.** For each verb, **two random samples of 100 COCA concordance lines** across all sections were coded for sense (verified, Users' Manual). Senses were added in frequency order until they covered **≥75%** of occurrences, and a sense needed **≥10%** to enter; when senses of ≥10% ran out before 75%, the entry stops below 75% (verified, paper p. 653).
- **Per-sense percentage and example.** Every sense has a definition, its percentage, and **one example sentence**, e.g. GO ON "Happen, take place (64.5%)" (verified, list PDF). The examples were **written by the authors** "to avoid possible copyright issues" with COCA, modelled on COCA and web sentences, and screened with Vocabprofile for rare words (verified, paper p. 654).
- **American.** Senses come from COCA; the manual says it "is reflective of mostly American English" (verified, Users' Manual). The 150 items were selected on COCA or BNC frequency.
- **Not graded for difficulty.** "No consideration was given to the meaning senses' varying degrees of semantic transparency or difficulty" (verified, Users' Manual).
- **Download.** norbertschmitt.co.uk/vocabulary-resources: the list, the article, the Users' Manual, an alphabetical index and a frequency ranking, all PDF (verified).
- **Licence.** **None stated** on the page (verified). The article is "© The Author(s) 2014", SAGE, with a permissions link (verified, PDF). The definitions and example sentences are the authors' own writing, so copying them into a CC BY-SA directory needs permission. The selection (Liu's 150) and the sense percentages are findings that can be cited.
- **Follow-up.** Garnier & Schmitt (2016): 128 students on BA English/TEFL courses took a productive gap-fill on PHaVE senses and knew **about 40%** of the senses on average; knowing every sense of a verb happened only about 20% of the time. Corpus frequency, weekly reading time and social networking time predicted knowledge; semantic opacity, prior instruction, immersion and year of study did not (abstract seen on Semantic Scholar; metadata verified via Crossref; L1 of the sample not checked). No later revised edition of the PHaVE List was found.

Refs:
- Garnier, M., & Schmitt, N. (2015). The PHaVE List: A pedagogical list of phrasal verbs and their most frequent meaning senses. *Language Teaching Research, 19*(6), 645–666. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168814559798 (verified, full text, list and manual read; online 2014)
- Garnier, M., & Schmitt, N. (2016). Picking up polysemous phrasal verbs: How many do learners know and what facilitates this knowledge? *System, 59*, 29–44. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2016.04.004 (metadata verified; abstract secondary)

## 4a. Value of multi-word units for learners

- **Processing.** Conklin & Schmitt (2008): native and nonnative speakers both read formulaic sequences (e.g. *take the bull by the horns*) faster than matched nonformulaic phrases, in idiomatic and literal uses alike (verified, publisher abstract). Siyanova-Chanturia, Conklin & van Heuven (2011), eye-tracking with binomials (*bride and groom* vs *groom and bride*): native and nonnative speakers across proficiencies are sensitive to phrase frequency; only natives and higher-proficiency nonnatives also showed the configuration effect (verified, abstract).
- **Proficiency.** Boers et al. (2006): 32 English majors, 22 teaching hours; the group taught to notice formulaic sequences was rated more proficient by blind judges, and formula counts correlated with the ratings (verified, abstract). Small sample.
- **Review.** Boers & Lindstromberg (2012) reviewed experimental and intervention studies since 2004: learners gain from a sizeable repertoire but are "slow to close the gap", and treatments fall into noticing, lookups or corpus tools, and memorisation (verified, abstract). No pooled effect size.
- **Overestimated comprehension.** Martinez & Murphy (2011): 101 adult Brazilian learners read short texts built from the top 2,000 words, once as free combinations and once with the same words arranged as multiword expressions (*large, and, by* → *by and large*). Comprehension "decreased significantly" with the expressions present, and learners "tended to overestimate how much they understood" because the expressions went unnoticed or were misread (verified, publisher abstract via Crossref). L1 Portuguese, also a Romance, verb-framed language. Kan & Murphy (2020) found the same drop in primary-school children, EAL and monolingual (verified, abstract; https://doi.org/10.14746/ssllt.2020.10.3.8).
- **Deceptive transparency.** Martinez & Schmitt (2012): in a sample academic text, words off the list rise from 7.46% to 26.87% once opaque phrases are counted; coverage for a 2K-level learner drops from 88.06% to 68.65% (verified, paper pp. 313–314). One text, a demonstration, not a study.
- **Wray (2002)** is the standard theoretical account (book; metadata verified, contents not read here).
- **Strength.** Moderate for faster processing and for the coverage argument; weak (small samples) for gains in speaking.

## 4b. How many different example contexts per word

- **Meta-analysis.** Chen, Miller & Ke (2026), 20 studies, 1,540 participants, L1 and L2: behavioural effect of contextual diversity on word learning **d = 0.44, p < .001**; eye-tracking shows longer processing under diversity during learning (gaze duration d = −0.574 as coded) (verified, abstract). From the article page: L1 subgroup d = 0.52 (22 samples), **L2 subgroup d = 0.60 from only 4 studies**, both heterogeneous; age was the moderator found; no eye-tracking study had a retention post-test; open access CC BY-NC 4.0 (verified, page read; the page's own Q statistics are inconsistent, as noted there).
- **Pagán & Nation (2019)**, adult L1 readers, eye-tracking: a word in **four different sentences** vs **one sentence four times**. Diverse words were learned more slowly but did better at the immediate posttest; retrieval helped most for words met in diverse contexts; spacing gave no posttest benefit (verified, abstract). Immediate test only.
- **Johns, Dye & Jones (2016)**, L1 adults, incidental exposure in real texts: words met across distinct discourse contexts were recognised **faster and more accurately**, but **redundant contexts gave more stable semantic representations** (verified, abstract).
- **Norman et al. (2023)**, preregistered, 239 L1 adults, 8 pseudowords × 10 sentences: diversity had **no effect on word-form learning**; for meaning, the non-diverse condition won in **familiar** contexts and the diverse condition won in **unfamiliar** contexts (verified, abstract).
- **Li et al. (2024)**, preregistered, 288 L1 adults, five anchoring exposures in one topic, then six topics vs one: **null on form recognition** again; low diversity won in familiar contexts; the diverse advantage in new contexts was **non-significant** (verified, author accepted manuscript abstract).
- **Mak, Hsiao & Nation (2021)**, L1 adults: Experiment 1, a single topic beat six topics; Experiment 2, after an anchoring phase, diversity won ("anchoring") (secondary, unverified: from search snippets and Norman et al.'s account).
- **Joseph & Nation (2018)**, L1 children aged 10–11: **no difference** between diverse and uniform contexts in reading times or offline posttests (verified, abstract).
- **Bolger, Balass, Landen & Perfetti (2008)**, L1 undergraduates, rare English words in the same sentence four times vs four different sentences, with and without definitions: reported to favour varied contexts for meaning; Norman et al. note it found no diversity effect on orthographic choice (secondary, unverified: abstract not read; publisher and lab PDF blocked).
- **Webb (2008)**, L2 (Japanese EFL university students), 10 words in 3 sets of 10 contexts: **more informative contexts** raised both meaning scores; the **number of encounters** mattered more for form (verified, abstract seen on Semantic Scholar; metadata verified). It manipulates context quality, not diversity.
- **Yang (2026)**, L2, 80 Chinese undergraduates, 36 words in varied vs repeated stories: varied input reported better on semantic generation at immediate and delayed tests, more so for larger vocabularies (secondary, unverified: search summary only; metadata verified).
- **Verdict.** Varied contexts help **meaning, and above all using the word in a new context**; they do **not** help form learning (two preregistered nulls); a single consistent context helps **meaning in that same context** and may anchor a new word before variation pays off. The evidence is mostly **L1 adults reading pseudowords incidentally**; L2 evidence is four studies in the meta-analysis plus Yang (2026). No study fixes an optimal number of frames; designs used 4 sentences (Pagán & Nation; Bolger et al.), 6 topics (Mak et al.; Li et al.) or 10 sentences (Norman et al.). Context informativeness (Webb) matters as much as the count.
- **Strength.** Moderate for meaning and generalisation; null for form; weak for L2 specifically.
- **Design implication.** One fixed, highly informative anchor frame first, then several different frames on later reviews, aimed at transfer to new sentences. Form (spelling) needs encounters and retrieval, not frame variety.

## 5. Phrasal verbs and Spanish L1 learners

- **Avoidance studies are on other L1s.** Dagut & Laufer (1985), Hebrew (no PVs in L1): frames avoidance as the reverse of negative transfer (verified, abstract opening; the result itself secondary). Laufer & Eliasson (1993), advanced Swedish learners (L1 has PVs): they "avoided neither phrasal verbs in general nor the figurative ones" (verified, abstract), which points to L1–L2 difference as the main cause (secondary: that conclusion is past the truncated abstract). Liao & Fukuya (2004), 70 Chinese learners: avoidance depended on proficiency, literal vs figurative PV and test type, read as interlanguage development (verified, abstract).
- **Spanish-specific.** No peer-reviewed avoidance experiment with Spanish L1 learners was found. Corpus evidence only: Alejo-González (2010) compared *out*-PVs across ICLE learners and reports that verb-framed L1 learners (Spanish, Italian) use fewer of them than satellite-framed L1 learners (Swedish, Dutch; Russian, Bulgarian) (metadata and design verified, abstract; result secondary). Alejo-González (Language Value 4(1), dated 2012 by its DOI, 2019 by Crossref): across six ICLE subcorpora including Spanish, *out* is underused, a few frequent verbs are overused, and forms are frozen (verified, abstract; results pooled, not per L1). Other Spanish-L1 work found is undergraduate or master's theses.
- **Inference, not a finding.** Spanish is verb-framed and has no phrasal verbs, so the Hebrew and Swedish contrast predicts avoidance or underuse by our players; no study tested that prediction directly for Spanish speakers.
