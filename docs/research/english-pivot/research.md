# Evidence brief: learning English as an L2, for an OGame-like strategy game with an L1 scaffold

Compiled 2026-11-04. Verification rule: a reference is **verified** when its title, authors, year, venue and DOI (or stable URL) were confirmed on a fetched page (Crossref API, publisher page, ACL Anthology, Europe PMC, author repository). Effect sizes are reported only when they appeared in a fetched abstract or full text; "n.r." means none was seen. **Unverified** marks anything not confirmed that way.

Strength scale: **strong** (several meta-analyses, consistent, includes L2), **moderate** (one meta-analysis or consistent experiments, with notable moderators or gaps), **weak** (few or small studies, or vendor-funded), **contested** (open theoretical or methodological dispute).

## 1. Spacing effect

- **Finding.** Distributed practice beats massed practice for verbal recall. The best inter-study gap grows with the retention interval (Cepeda et al., 2006: 839 assessments, 317 experiments, 184 articles). In a 1,350+ participant study with gaps up to 3.5 months and tests up to 1 year later, the optimal gap as a share of the test delay fell from about 20–40% for a 1-week delay to about 5–10% for a 1-year delay: the "temporal ridgeline" (Cepeda et al., 2008).
- **L2-specific.** Kim & Webb (2022): 98 effect sizes, 48 experiments, N = 3,411. Spacing had a "medium-to-large effect" on L2 learning. Short and long gaps were similar on immediate posttests; longer gaps won on delayed posttests. **Equal and expanding schedules did not differ.** Moderators: learning target, number of sessions, type of practice, activity type, feedback timing, retention interval.
- **Effect size.** n.r. as a number in the abstracts seen.
- **Strength.** Strong.
- **Caveats.** Most of the data is lab-based and on vocabulary or paired associates. The optimal gap depends on how long you want the item kept, so there is no single best interval. That expanding intervals did not beat equal intervals in L2 weakens one common app assumption.
- **Design implication.** Schedule reviews across days, not within a session. Tie the gap to the retention goal. Do not sell expanding intervals as proven better than fixed ones.

Refs:
- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin, 132*(3), 354–380. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354 (verified)
- Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. *Psychological Science, 19*(11), 1095–1102. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x (verified; volume and pages unverified)
- Kim, S. K., & Webb, S. (2022). The effects of spaced practice on second language learning: A meta-analysis. *Language Learning*. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479 (verified; volume and pages not checked)

## 2. Retrieval practice (testing effect)

- **Finding.** Taking a test beats restudying for delayed retention, even though restudying looks better at 5 minutes (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006: final tests at 5 min, 2 days, 1 week).
- **Meta-analytic size.** Rowland (2014), random effects, testing vs restudy: **g = 0.50, 95% CI [0.42, 0.58]**, I² = 84 (high heterogeneity). With feedback g = 0.73 [0.61, 0.86]; without feedback g = 0.39. Retention interval of 1 day or more g = 0.69; under 1 day g = 0.41. **Paired associates** (the format of L1–L2 word pairs) g = 0.59 [0.49, 0.70]; single words g = 0.33. Recall tests as the initial test gave larger benefits than recognition tests. All numbers taken from the full text.
- Adesope et al. (2017): practice tests are "more beneficial for learning than restudying and all other comparison conditions", with moderators for test format, participant and study features, and outcome. Coverage: 118 articles and 272 effect sizes per secondary sources (unverified). Effect sizes n.r. in the abstract seen.
- **Strength.** Strong. For L2 vocabulary, moderate-to-strong: paired associates are the best-supported stimulus type, but most of those studies are lab studies, not L2 classrooms.
- **Caveats.** Benefits grow with delay and feedback. Recall formats (typing or saying the word) beat recognition formats (multiple choice).
- **Design implication.** The core loop should make the player *produce* the answer (L1→L2 and L2→L1 recall) and give immediate corrective feedback. Use multiple choice only as an on-ramp.

Refs:
- Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science, 17*(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x (verified; volume and pages unverified)
- Rowland, C. A. (2014). The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect. *Psychological Bulletin, 140*(6), 1432–1463. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559 (verified, full text read)
- Adesope, O. O., Trevisan, D. A., & Sundararajan, N. (2017). Rethinking the use of tests: A meta-analysis of practice testing. *Review of Educational Research, 87*(3), 659–701. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306 (verified; numeric g unverified, so not reported)

## 3. Interleaving

- **Finding.** Brunmair & Richter (2019): 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples. Overall **g = 0.42**. Paintings g = 0.67; maths g = 0.34. **Words g = −0.39: blocking beat interleaving.** Expository texts n.s. Interleaving helps more when categories look alike, when items within a category look different, and when the material is complex.
- **Strength.** Moderate in general; **weak or negative for vocabulary**.
- **Design implication.** Do not interleave word lists in the hope of a gain. Interleaving may fit discrimination tasks between confusable forms (tense, aspect, prepositions); test it before relying on it.

Ref: Brunmair, M., & Richter, T. (2019). Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators. *Psychological Bulletin*. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209 (verified)

## 4. Frequency-based vocabulary and lexical coverage

- **Coverage thresholds.** Nation (2006): with 98% coverage taken as the target, reading needs **8,000–9,000 word families** and listening **6,000–7,000**. Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010): **98% coverage needs about 8,000 families** (optimal threshold); **95% needs about 4,000–5,000** (minimal threshold). Hu & Nation (2000) is the source of the 98% figure: one 633-word narrative at 80/90/95/100% coverage.
- **Contested.** Kremmel, Indrarathne, Kormos & Suzuki (2023) note that the 98% figure rests on a regression with 66 New Zealand university students. Their replication (104 Sri Lankan adults) "could not be fully replicated" the original. The figures are useful heuristics, not hard thresholds.
- **High-frequency lists.** NGSL (Browne, Culligan & Phillips, 2013): v1.2 (2023) has **2,809 words**. The authors claim "92%+" coverage of general English. That is a vendor claim from the list's own site; no independent replication was fetched. Dang & Webb (2016) compared GSL, BNC2000, BNC/COCA2000 and New-GSL across 18 corpora: the BNC/COCA2000 headwords gave the greatest average coverage, while New-GSL led at the top 1,000/1,500/1,996 headwords. Dang, Webb & Coxhead (2020): teachers rated BNC/COCA2000 more useful, and learners knew more of its items. **Name clash:** the "New-GSL" of Brezina & Gablasova (2013) is a different list from Browne's NGSL.
- **Strength.** Strong for "frequency predicts usefulness and coverage". The exact 95/98% thresholds and family counts are moderate or contested.
- **Design implication.** Order content by frequency band: first 1,000–2,000 (BNC/COCA2000 or NGSL), then up to 5k, then up to 8–9k for unassisted reading. Use 95% coverage as the floor for assisted reading with glosses and 98% for free reading. Do not hard-code one magic number.

Refs:
- Nation, I. S. P. (2006). How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening? *Canadian Modern Language Review, 63*(1), 59–82. https://doi.org/10.3138/cmlr.63.1.59 (verified; pages unverified)
- Laufer, B., & Ravenhorst-Kalovski, G. C. (2010). Lexical threshold revisited: Lexical text coverage, learners' vocabulary size and reading comprehension. *Reading in a Foreign Language, 22*(1). https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/66648 (verified as Crossref lists it; volume unverified)
- Hu, M., & Nation, I. S. P. (2000). Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension. *Reading in a Foreign Language, 13*(1), 403–430. https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/43 (verified via search metadata; abstract not read)
- Kremmel, B., Indrarathne, B., Kormos, J., & Suzuki, S. (2023). Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension: Replicating Hu and Nation (2000). *Language Learning*. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12622 (verified)
- Browne, C., Culligan, B., & Phillips, J. (2013). *The New General Service List*. https://www.newgeneralservicelist.com/new-general-service-list (verified as a web resource; not peer-reviewed)

## 5. Comprehensible input and extensive reading

- **Krashen's input hypothesis.** Its weak form (input is necessary and central) is widely accepted. Its strong form (comprehensible input is *sufficient*, and teaching form does not help) is **contested**: critics call "i+1" undefined and the claim unfalsifiable, and the output, interaction and feedback literatures below argue against sufficiency. No primary critique was fetched; the criticism was seen only in secondary sources.
- **Extensive reading (ER).** Nakanishi (2015): 34 studies, 43 effect sizes, N = 3,942. Group contrasts **d = 0.46**; pre–post **d = 0.71**. Jeon & Day (2016): "small to medium effect" on reading proficiency in both study designs; numbers n.r. in the abstract.
- **Incidental vocabulary.** Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019): correlational meta-analysis, 26 studies, 45 effect sizes, N = 1,918. Frequency of encounters correlates with incidental learning at **r = .34** (medium). Learning from reading alone is slow and needs many repetitions.
- **Strength.** ER: moderate. Incidental learning grows with repetition: moderate. Strong-form Krashen: contested.
- **Design implication.** Graded in-fiction texts (reports, chronicles, letters) at about 95–98% known-word coverage, with high-frequency targets recurring across texts. Pair them with explicit retrieval of the target words; do not rely on input alone.

Refs:
- Krashen, S. D. (1985). *The input hypothesis: Issues and implications*. Longman. (unverified: book, no page fetched)
- Nakanishi, T. (2015). A meta-analysis of extensive reading research. *TESOL Quarterly, 49*(1), 6–37. https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.157 (verified; online 2014; d values from secondary indexes, consistent across two)
- Jeon, E.-Y., & Day, R. R. (2016). The effectiveness of ER on reading proficiency: A meta-analysis. *Reading in a Foreign Language, 28*(2). https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/66901 (verified; issue unverified)
- Uchihara, T., Webb, S., & Yanagisawa, A. (2019). The effects of repetition on incidental vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis of correlational studies. *Language Learning*. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12343 (verified)

## 6. Output (Swain) and interaction (Long)

- **Finding.** The output hypothesis holds that producing language makes learners notice gaps and test hypotheses. The interaction hypothesis holds that negotiating meaning plus feedback drives acquisition. Mackey & Goo (2007) meta-analysed interaction studies (about 26–28 by secondary counts) and are reported to find large effects for interaction vs no interaction, larger in lab than classroom studies, including lexis. Numbers were not seen in a primary source.
- **Strength.** Moderate.
- **Caveats.** Lab effects are larger than classroom effects, and outcomes are often short-term.
- **Design implication.** Require output: typed or spoken orders, diplomacy messages, negotiation with NPCs or other players, all of which feed into the feedback loop of section 7.

Refs:
- Swain, M. (1985). Communicative competence: Some roles of comprehensible input and comprehensible output in its development. In S. Gass & C. Madden (Eds.), *Input in second language acquisition* (pp. 235–253). Newbury House. (unverified)
- Long, M. H. (1996). The role of the linguistic environment in second language acquisition. In W. C. Ritchie & T. K. Bhatia (Eds.), *Handbook of second language acquisition* (pp. 413–468). Academic Press. (unverified)
- Mackey, A., & Goo, J. (2007). Interaction research in SLA: A meta-analysis and research synthesis. In A. Mackey (Ed.), *Conversational interaction in second language acquisition* (pp. 407–452). Oxford University Press. https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/59863/ (verified via repository and search metadata; effect sizes unverified)
- Gass, S. M., & Mackey, A. (2006). Input, interaction and output: An overview. *AILA Review, 19*. https://doi.org/10.1075/aila.19.03gas (verified; background)

## 7. Corrective feedback

- **Finding.** Li (2010): 33 studies. Corrective feedback has a **medium, durable** effect. Implicit feedback was retained better over time than explicit feedback. Lab > classroom, foreign-language > second-language settings, shorter treatments > longer ones. Lyster & Saito (2010): 15 classroom studies, N = 827. Effects were "significant and durable". **Prompts (which push the learner to self-correct) beat recasts (which simply reformulate)**, most clearly on free-production measures. Younger learners benefited more. Russell & Spada (2006) is the early grammar-focused synthesis. In a single experiment, Ellis, Loewen & Erlam (2006) found that explicit metalinguistic feedback beat recasts on delayed tests.
- **Effect size.** n.r. numerically in the abstracts seen.
- **Strength.** Strong that feedback works. The question of which type of feedback works best is moderate to contested: Li's implicit-durability result and the advantage of prompts and explicit feedback pull in different directions.
- **Design implication.** On an error, first prompt the player to self-correct (a hint, or a metalinguistic cue in the L1), then give the answer. Log the type of error so the scheduler can resurface the item.

Refs:
- Li, S. (2010). The effectiveness of corrective feedback in SLA: A meta-analysis. *Language Learning, 60*(2). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x (verified; issue unverified)
- Lyster, R., & Saito, K. (2010). Oral feedback in classroom SLA: A meta-analysis. *Studies in Second Language Acquisition, 32*(2), 265–302. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263109990520 (verified)
- Russell, J., & Spada, N. (2006). The effectiveness of corrective feedback for the acquisition of L2 grammar: A meta-analysis of the research. In J. Norris & L. Ortega (Eds.), *Synthesizing research on language learning and teaching* (pp. 133–164). John Benjamins. https://doi.org/10.1075/lllt.13.09val (DOI verified; Crossref lists the author as "Jane Russell Valezy"; editors and pages unverified)

## 8. Task-based language teaching (TBLT)

The topic text's "Bryfonce" is almost certainly **Bryfonski**, a real author.

- **Finding.** Bryfonski & McKay (2019; online 2017): 52 studies, overall **d = 0.93** for TBLT programmes. Xuan, Cheung & Liu (2022) re-analysed with stricter inclusion criteria and got **g = 0.61**; only sample size and teacher-as-researcher explained heterogeneity. Boers & Faez (2023) conclude the field is "not ripe yet for a meaningful meta-analysis" of TBLT programmes because "task" and "task-supported" are poorly defined. Boers, Bryfonski, Faez & McKay (2021) call for cautious reading of meta-analyses in general.
- **Strength.** Moderate that the effect is positive; **contested** on its size.
- **Design implication.** A strategy game is a natural task generator: goal-oriented, meaning-first, with an outcome (trade, alliance, scouting report). Use tasks as the context, but keep the retrieval and feedback machinery of sections 2 and 7 inside them.

Refs:
- Bryfonski, L., & McKay, T. H. (2019). TBLT implementation and evaluation: A meta-analysis. *Language Teaching Research, 23*(5). https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168817744389 (verified; online 2017; issue unverified)
- Xuan, Q., Cheung, A., & Liu, J. (2022). How effective is task-based language teaching to enhance second language learning? A technical comment on Bryfonski and McKay (2019). *Language Teaching Research*. https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221131127 (verified)
- Boers, F., & Faez, F. (2023). Meta-analysis to estimate the relative effectiveness of TBLT programs: Are we there yet? *Language Teaching Research*. https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688231167573 (verified)
- Boers, F., Bryfonski, L., Faez, F., & McKay, T. (2021). A call for cautious interpretation of meta-analytic reviews. *SSLA*. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263120000327 (verified; Crossref year 2020)

## 9. Dual coding and multimedia glosses

- **Finding.** Dual coding theory: verbal plus imagery codes give two retrieval routes (Clark & Paivio, 1991). Yun (2011, CALL hypertext glosses): text plus visual glosses beat text alone, weighted mean **d = 0.46** across 10 studies according to the thesis abstract; Boulton's table lists 0.37, so the exact value is uncertain. Ramezanali, Uchihara & Faez (2021): 22 studies, 26 effect sizes. Adding a gloss mode gave **g = 0.46** on immediate posttests and **g = 0.28** on delayed posttests.
- **Strength.** Moderate; the effect shrinks at delay.
- **Design implication.** Concrete nouns (units, buildings, resources) get an image plus the word plus audio. Do not expect images to replace retrieval practice.

Refs:
- Clark, J. M., & Paivio, A. (1991). Dual coding theory and education. *Educational Psychology Review, 3*(3). https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01320076 (verified; volume unverified)
- Yun, J. (2011). The effects of hypertext glosses on L2 vocabulary acquisition: A meta-analysis. *Computer Assisted Language Learning, 24*(1). https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2010.523285 (verified; effect size partly unverified, see above)
- Ramezanali, N., Uchihara, T., & Faez, F. (2021). Efficacy of multimodal glossing on second language vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis. *TESOL Quarterly*. https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.579 (verified; Crossref year 2020, online first)

## 10. L1 scaffolding: glosses, translation pairs, own-language use, fading

- **L1 vs L2 glosses.** Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara (2020): 42 studies, 359 effect sizes, N = 3,802. Glossed reading beat unglossed reading: **45.3% vs 26.6%** of words learned on immediate posttests and **33.4% vs 19.8%** on delayed posttests. **L1 glosses beat L2 glosses.** Multiple-choice glosses were the most effective type; in-text glosses and glossaries the least effective. Laufer & Shmueli (1997): words glossed in the L1 were retained better than words glossed in English, and lists or single sentences beat full texts.
- **Intentional word-focused activities.** Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020): 22 studies, 100 effect sizes. Immediate gains were about **60.1%** (meaning recall) and **58.5%** (form recall); delayed gains about **39.4%** and **25.1%**. Gains varied widely by activity, from 18.4% to 77.0% immediate. Learning direction (L1→L2 vs L2→L1) and learning context moderated the results.
- **Own-language use.** Hall & Cook (2012) review the case for principled own-language use in teaching, a reversal of the monolingual orthodoxy. It is a narrative review, not a meta-analysis; its abstract was not read.
- **Fading.** Expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller, 2003): guidance that helps novices becomes redundant and can hurt as expertise grows. That is a direct argument for fading the L1 scaffold per item and per learner, based on measured mastery rather than on the calendar. No L2-specific study of the right fade point was found.
- **Strength.** Strong for L1 glosses and translation pairs in the early stages. Moderate for fading; the principle is robust, but there is no L2 parameterisation.
- **Design implication.** The L1 scaffold is evidence-backed, not a crutch: L1 glosses, L1↔L2 recall pairs, and L1 metalinguistic hints. Fade each item's scaffold once retrieval is stable: L1 gloss → L2 definition or image → none. Let a player re-summon the scaffold, and treat a re-summon as a lapse signal for the scheduler.

Refs:
- Yanagisawa, A., Webb, S., & Uchihara, T. (2020). How do different forms of glossing contribute to L2 vocabulary learning from reading? A meta-regression analysis. *SSLA*. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263119000688 (verified)
- Laufer, B., & Shmueli, K. (1997). Memorizing new words: Does teaching have anything to do with it? *RELC Journal, 28*(1). https://doi.org/10.1177/003368829702800106 (verified)
- Webb, S., Yanagisawa, A., & Uchihara, T. (2020). How effective are intentional vocabulary-learning activities? A meta-analysis. *Modern Language Journal*. https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12671 (verified)
- Hall, G., & Cook, G. (2012). Own-language use in language teaching and learning. *Language Teaching, 45*(3). https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444812000067 (verified; volume unverified)
- Kalyuga, S., Ayres, P., Chandler, P., & Sweller, J. (2003). The expertise reversal effect. *Educational Psychologist, 38*(1). https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4 (verified)

## 11. Gamified and mobile apps

- **Duolingo.** Vesselinov & Grego (2012): a report, not peer-reviewed, **funded by Duolingo**. It estimated about 34 hours (26–49) of use as equivalent to a first college semester of Spanish. Secondary sources report that 90 of 156 starters finished (high attrition, self-selected volunteers; unverified, primary PDF not opened). Loewen et al. (2019, ReCALL): **9 participants**, Turkish, one semester; gains moderately correlated with time on the app; mixed motivation and frustration with materials. Jiang, Rollinson, Plonsky, Gustafson & Pajak (2021, FLA): 225 US adults finished beginner Spanish or French courses and reached ACTFL Intermediate Low in reading and Novice High in listening, comparable to the end of a 4th university semester. Caveats: only reading and listening were tested; completers only (survivorship); four of the five authors appear to be Duolingo staff (affiliations not verified on a fetched page).
- **Babbel.** Loewen, Isbell & Sporn (2020, FLA): 54 completers (out of 85 starters per the MSU press release, so about 36% quit) used Babbel for 12 weeks. Grammar, vocabulary and oral ability improved; study time was the strongest predictor. Funded by Lesson Nine GmbH (Babbel's operating company), and Sporn is affiliated with Babbel.
- **MALL.** Burston (2015): of 291 MALL studies, 35 met minimum duration and sample criteria; 16 of those had serious design flaws; of the 19 remaining, 15 were positive. The 4 null results were all vocabulary studies. "Statistically reliable measures of learning outcomes are few and far between."
- **Gamification meta-analyses.** Sailer & Homner (2020): cognitive **g = .49** [0.30, 0.69] (k = 19), motivational **g = .36** [0.18, 0.54] (k = 16), and g = .25 [0.04, 0.46] (k = 9), which is likely the behavioural outcome (label inferred from a fragment). Only the cognitive effect was stable when restricted to rigorous studies (per a secondary summary). Bai, Hew & Huang (2020): **g = 0.504** [0.284, 0.723], 30 interventions, 24 studies, N = 3,202.
- **Strength.** Gamification adds a small-to-medium effect: moderate. App efficacy: weak (vendor-funded, completers only, no randomised controls). Novelty and attrition are the main threats.
- **Design implication.** Expect most players to quit. Measure learning on **all starters (intention to treat)**, not on completers. Track retention at 30 and 90 days, and run randomised A/B tests of learning features from day one. Game fiction and social interaction moderated behavioural outcomes in Sailer & Homner, which suits an OGame-like multiplayer frame.

Refs:
- Vesselinov, R., & Grego, J. (2012). *Duolingo effectiveness study: Final report*. Duolingo-funded report. https://static.duolingo.com/s3/DuolingoReport_Final.pdf (title, authors and funding verified via search snippets; PDF not opened, so partly unverified)
- Loewen, S., Crowther, D., Isbell, D. R., Kim, K. M., Maloney, J., Miller, Z. F., & Rawal, H. (2019). Mobile-assisted language learning: A Duolingo case study. *ReCALL, 31*(3), 293–311. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344019000065 (verified; funding not checked)

## 12. Motivation

- **Self-determination theory (SDT).** Supporting autonomy, competence and relatedness fosters internalised motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Noels et al. (2000) validated an SDT scale for L2 learning.
- **Overjustification.** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), 128 studies: engagement-contingent rewards **d = −0.40**, completion-contingent **d = −0.36** and performance-contingent **d = −0.28** on free-choice intrinsic motivation. Positive feedback *raised* free-choice behaviour (**d = 0.33**) and self-reported interest (**d = 0.31**).
- **L2 Motivational Self System.** Dörnyei (2009): ideal L2 self, ought-to L2 self, L2 learning experience. Al-Hoorie (2018), 32 reports, N = 32,078: ideal L2 self correlates **r = .61** with *intended effort* but only **r = .20** with *actual achievement*; ought-to self r = .38 with effort and −.05 with achievement; learning experience r = .41 with effort and .17 with achievement.
- **Strength.** Overjustification: strong for tangible, expected rewards. SDT: strong as a framework. The L2MSS predicts self-reported effort far better than achievement: moderate, and the gap is itself a caution.
- **Design implication.** Do not pay players in-game resources merely for completing lessons; that is exactly the engagement- and completion-contingent pattern with a negative d. Better: language competence *unlocks capability* (diplomacy, trade, intelligence) and the player gets informative competence feedback. Make social play the source of relatedness. Never use self-reported motivation as a proxy for learning.

Refs:
- Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. *American Psychologist, 55*(1), 68–78. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68 (verified)

## 13. Learning styles (rejected)

- **Finding.** Pashler et al. (2008) found "virtually no evidence" for the interaction pattern (matching instruction to a learner's style improves outcomes) that would be needed to justify learning-styles assessment in education. Few studies even used a design that could test it.
- **Strength.** Strong *against* style-matching.
- **Design implication.** No "visual / auditory learner" settings. Offer multimodality (section 9) to everyone.

Ref: Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008). Learning styles: Concepts and evidence. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 9*(3), 105–119. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x (verified)

## 14. Spaced-repetition schedulers

| Scheduler | What it is | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| Leitner boxes | Cards move up a box on success and back to box 1 on failure | Heuristic; original: Leitner, S. (1972). *So lernt man lernen*. (unverified) |
| SM-2 | Wozniak, 1987–1990: ease factor starts at 2.5 (min 1.3), intervals 1 d, 6 d, then I×EF, grades 0–5, grade < 3 restarts the item | Heuristic, from a master's thesis; not validated against data. Source: https://super-memory.com/english/ol/sm2.htm (verified) |
| HLR | Settles & Meeder (2016, ACL), Duolingo: half-life regression, "reducing error by 45%+" vs baselines at predicting recall, "+12% daily student engagement" in an operational study | **Peer-reviewed** (ACL); the engagement result is an in-house A/B test. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/P16-1174 (verified, abstract read) |
| SSP-MMC | Ye, Su & Cao (2022, KDD), MaiMemo: Markov memory model fitted on 220M logs; stochastic-shortest-path scheduler minimising review cost; "12.6% performance improvement" over state of the art | **Peer-reviewed** (KDD); company data; dataset public. https://doi.org/10.1145/3534678.3539081 (verified; 12.6% and 220M from search snippets of the abstract) |
| FSRS (v4–v7) | Open-source successor to the KDD model, the default scheduler in Anki | **Benchmark-only.** The open-spaced-repetition srs-benchmark (about 10k Anki collections, hundreds of millions of reviews; log loss, binned RMSE, AUC) ranks FSRS-6/7 best among non-neural models; RWKV neural models beat it. The benchmark is community-run, not peer-reviewed, and measures *prediction* of recall, not learning gains. https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark (verified) |

- **Design implication.** Use an FSRS-style model: open, fitted to data and the best-benchmarked non-neural option. Schedule at a target retention (for example 0.85–0.9). Remember that recall prediction accuracy ≠ learning outcome. Since the server is authoritative, compute due-ness lazily from (stability, difficulty, last_review, now), which matches ADR 005.

## 15. Placement and assessment

- **Vocabulary Size Test (VST).** Nation & Beglar (2007): 140 multiple-choice items, 10 per 1,000-word level up to the 14th 1,000; score × 100 ≈ word families known.
- **Updated Vocabulary Levels Test (UVLT).** Webb, Sasao & Ballance (2017): two forms, five levels (1k–5k) drawn from the BNC/COCA lists; trialled with 1,463 + 250 test takers and initially validated under Messick's framework. It tells the learner *which band to study*.
- **CEFR.** Council of Europe (2020) Companion Volume: updates the 2001 descriptors and adds mediation, online interaction and plurilingual scales.
- **Strength.** Moderate: validated instruments, but not computer-adaptive in their published form. An adaptive version is the designer's own extrapolation.
- **Design implication.** Onboarding: a short adaptive yes/no or meaning-recognition probe per frequency band (UVLT-style) to place the player's starting band. Report progress as "words known per band" and map it loosely to CEFR. Never claim a CEFR certification.

Refs:
- Nation, P., & Beglar, D. (2007). A vocabulary size test. *The Language Teacher, 31*(7), 9–13. https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/journal_contribution/A_vocabulary_size_test/12552197 (verified)
- Webb, S., Sasao, Y., & Ballance, O. (2017). The updated Vocabulary Levels Test. *ITL – International Journal of Applied Linguistics, 168*(1). https://doi.org/10.1075/itl.168.1.02web (verified)
- Council of Europe. (2020). *Common European Framework of Reference for Languages: Learning, teaching, assessment – Companion volume*. https://rm.coe.int/16809ea0d4 (verified via search; PDF not opened)

## Weak or contested evidence

- **Krashen's strong input hypothesis** (input is sufficient): contested; output, interaction and feedback evidence argue against sufficiency.
- **The 98% coverage threshold:** rests on a small sample, and the Kremmel et al. (2023) replication did not fully reproduce it. Treat 95/98% as heuristics.
- **NGSL "92%+ coverage":** the authors' own claim; independent comparisons (Dang & Webb) favour BNC/COCA2000 on average coverage.
- **TBLT effect size:** d = 0.93 fell to g = 0.61 on re-analysis, and Boers & Faez say a meaningful meta-analysis is not yet possible.
- **Implicit vs explicit feedback:** Li (2010) found implicit feedback more durable; Lyster & Saito (2010) and Ellis et al. (2006) favour prompts and explicit feedback. Unresolved.
- **Expanding vs uniform spacing:** no difference in L2 (Kim & Webb, 2022), despite app folklore.
- **Interleaving for vocabulary:** negative.
- **App efficacy (Duolingo, Babbel):** vendor-funded or vendor-co-authored, completers only, no randomised controls, 36%+ attrition where reported, small samples (n = 9 in Loewen et al., 2019).
- **Gamification:** the motivational and behavioural effects are less stable than the cognitive one; novelty effects are likely in short studies.
- **MALL:** most studies fail basic rigour (19 of 291 usable in Burston, 2015); vocabulary studies were the null ones.
- **SRS algorithm benchmarks:** they measure recall prediction on Anki logs, not learning gains, and are not peer-reviewed for FSRS. HLR's +12% is engagement, not learning.
- **L2 Motivational Self System:** predicts intended effort (r = .61), barely predicts achievement (r = .20).
- **Learning styles:** rejected.
